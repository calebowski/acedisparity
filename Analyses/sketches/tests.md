Let me check your actual repo to see how you're currently calling `treats`, so I can point to exactly what needs to change rather than giving generic advice.Good instinct — that low a Colless index is a real signal, and it's very likely a default-settings issue rather than something wrong with your setup.

**Why this happens:** `treats`' built-in birth-death algorithm works in three steps: draw a branch length (exponential, scaled by current lineage count and rates), pick a living lineage to act on **uniformly at random**, then decide speciation vs. extinction by comparing a random draw to the speciation/turnover ratio. With fixed λ and μ applied identically to every lineage, that's essentially a Yule/equal-rates-Markov (ERM) process — and ERM trees are well known to come out considerably more balanced than real phylogenies (this gap between the homogeneous-rates null and observed tree shape goes back to Mooers & Heard 1997, and is a standard motivation for adding rate heterogeneity to diversification models). A corrected Colless index under 0.15 is entirely consistent with that: you probably don't have a bug, you have a homogeneous-rates process, and homogeneous-rates processes just don't generate much imbalance on their own.

**The fix: make speciation propensity heritable rather than uniform.** Real trees are imbalanced because some lineages are more prone to diversifying than their sisters, and that propensity tends to run in clades. `treats` gives you a direct route to this via `make.modifiers()`, which lets you rewrite any of the three algorithm steps above instead of using the defaults. Two useful levers:

1. **Bias which lineage gets selected next**, so lineages with a certain (evolving) trait value are more likely to be picked to speciate. `treats` has a `parent.traits()` helper specifically for pulling a lineage's own or its ancestor's trait value inside a modifier, so you can weight selection by trait value instead of sampling uniformly.
2. **Make λ (and/or μ) itself a function of a trait**, via `make.bd.params()` combined with a modifier — each lineage's speciation rate depends on where it sits on some evolving axis, similar in spirit to a BiSSE-style state-dependent model, just simulated forward instead of fit backward.

The trait driving the rate doesn't have to be one of your morphological characters — you can add a hidden "diversification propensity" trait purely for this purpose (evolving under BM, say) so it doesn't contaminate the actual character simulations.

**A nice side benefit:** this is functionally the same fix that would let you engage with Wagner's "super-progenitor" point. A lineage that's heritably more prone to speciating is, in his argument, also more likely to accumulate fossil finds over its longer functional "reach." Building heritable rate heterogeneity in for the imbalance problem gives you a natural hook for testing that part of his critique too, rather than treating them as two unrelated fixes.

**Two lower-priority things worth ruling out:**
- Your `stop.rule` — if you're conditioning simulations on hitting an exact target extant-tip count (rejecting and re-running otherwise, per your Methods), it's worth checking this isn't contributing. Easy to test: compare Colless values from a run stopped on fixed simulation time instead of a fixed tip count.
- What you're benchmarking 0.15 against — pure ERM trees aren't perfectly balanced either, so it's worth confirming that's genuinely low relative to the ERM expectation at your specific tree sizes, not just low relative to intuition.
